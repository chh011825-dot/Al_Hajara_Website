Home Construction Costs in Karachi: What to Budget For

AL-HAJARA Estate & Builders · 8 min read

Almost every client who comes to us with a fixed construction budget ends up spending more than they first planned — not because of bad luck, but because the initial number they had in mind only covered part of the build. Grey structure is only the beginning. Here's a realistic way to think about where the money actually goes.

The four stages of cost

StageWhat it covers
Grey structureFoundation, columns, beams, slabs, brick or block walls, roofing
Grey plusPlumbing lines, electrical wiring, plastering, damp-proofing
FinishingFlooring, tiling, paint, doors, windows, kitchen and bathroom fittings
Fit-outBuilt-in cabinetry, false ceilings, light fixtures, landscaping

Most quotes clients see advertised only cover the first stage — grey structure. It looks like the biggest number, so it feels like "most of the cost," but finishing and fit-out together typically add just as much, if not more, to the final bill.

Where first-time builders under-budget

  • Import-dependent finishes. Imported tiles, sanitaryware and fittings fluctuate with currency exchange rates. If your finishing list leans heavily on imported brands, price it in dollars, not rupees, and re-check closer to purchase.
  • Utility connections. Gas, electricity, and water connection charges, meters, and any society development or possession-linked charges are frequently left out of the "construction" budget entirely.
  • Site access and logistics. A plot with difficult site access (narrow lane, no direct road for material delivery) can add real cost in labour and transport that a standard quote won't anticipate.
  • Design changes mid-build. Every change made after grey structure is poured costs more than the same change made on paper. Finalise the drawing before construction starts, not during it.
A useful rule of thumb: whatever your grey structure quote is, plan for it to represent roughly 45–55% of your total build cost — not the whole project.

Fixed-price vs. cost-plus contracts

Contractors generally offer one of two models. A fixed-price contract locks in the total cost against an agreed specification sheet — you know exactly what you're paying, provided you don't change the spec. A cost-plus contract bills materials and labour as they're used, plus a margin — more flexible, but harder to predict. For first-time builders, a fixed-price contract against a clearly written specification sheet is almost always the safer choice.

What a proper specification sheet should include

  • Brand and grade of cement, steel and bricks being used
  • Exact tile, sanitaryware and fitting brands with model numbers, not just "standard" or "premium"
  • Paint brand and number of coats
  • Electrical wiring gauge and switch/socket brand
  • A written payment schedule tied to physical milestones, not calendar dates

If a contractor is unwilling to put this in writing before you sign, treat that as the biggest red flag in the entire process.
